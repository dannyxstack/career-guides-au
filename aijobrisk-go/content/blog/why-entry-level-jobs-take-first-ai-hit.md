A recurring theme in recent coverage is that AI is hitting the *bottom* of the
ladder first. Korean reporting has highlighted that a striking share of youth
job losses is concentrated in AI-intensive sectors; a D&AD report warns that AI
may be cutting the junior creative jobs that build professional judgement; and in
the US, recent graduates say AI is making entry-level roles harder to find — even
as economists debate how much of that is really AI.

## Junior work is the most exposed work

This is not a coincidence. Entry-level roles are defined by exactly the tasks
today's models do best: drafting first-pass copy, cleaning data, producing
routine designs, summarising documents, basic bookkeeping. In our exposure data,
these task-heavy roles — technical writers, junior designers, data-entry and
bookkeeping work — sit toward the high end of generative-AI exposure.

## The hidden cost: the training pipeline

The near-term efficiency is obvious; the long-term cost is not. Those junior
tasks were never only output — they were how people *learned*. A designer earns
judgement by doing the unglamorous work first; an analyst learns the business by
cleaning its data. Automating the bottom rung risks starving the pipeline that
produces tomorrow's senior professionals. That is the real warning in the D&AD
findings, not just a headcount number.

## What it means if you are early-career

Exposure is not the same as elimination. The resilient move is to climb toward
the parts of the job AI does *not* do well: client judgement, ambiguous problems,
accountability for outcomes. The occupation pages on this site show, per role,
which tasks are exposed and where the human moat is — a useful map for deciding
what to double down on.

## Takeaway

AI is compressing the entry level, and that shows up clearly in the exposure
data. The individual response is to move up the judgement curve fast; the
industry problem — who trains the next generation — is still unsolved.
