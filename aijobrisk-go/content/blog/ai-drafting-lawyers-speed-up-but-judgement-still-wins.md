Many lawyers now use generative AI to produce first drafts of contracts, memos, and discovery requests. A tool trained on legal text can assemble a contract clause or a case summary in seconds. For a busy associate, that saves hours. What used to take an afternoon can become a starting point for review.

But the phrase “starting point” matters. AI drafting is not legal advice. It is pattern recognition at scale. It predicts language based on training data, and it can be fluent and credible. It can also cite cases that do not exist, omit a vital jurisdiction-specific requirement, or miss the political and practical context that shapes a legal negotiation. This is why the same lawyers who are comfortable delegating a first draft to AI still want a human to sign off.

## Why routine legal drafting exposes AI’s strengths

Legal work has many repetitive, text-heavy tasks. Reviewing boxes of documents for relevance, standardising lease clauses, preparing Freedom of Information requests, and compiling routine due diligence reports all involve reading and writing within well-defined formats. Generative AI handles these well because the task structure is predictable and success is relatively easy to evaluate.

In our task-exposure framework, this is the kind of task we classify as high exposure. The core activity is language transformation: if a human supplies the input, a model can produce a serviceable first cut. Legal services firms, in sectors such as professional services and finance, are already feeling the effect in their support roles.

## Measuring AI exposure for legal occupations

At AIJobRisk, we look beyond job titles. A lawyer’s role is a bundle of tasks, and each task has its own exposure level. For an occupation like lawyer, some tasks carry high exposure to generative AI. Drafting legal briefs, summarising depositions, and performing legal research are near-universal in the profession. Those map closely to what large language models can do.

Other tasks pull the average down. Advising a client on how a new regulation affects a specific merger involves judgement about risk appetite, business context and the client’s broader objectives. Representing a client in court, mediating a dispute, or negotiating a settlement with opposing counsel requires in-person reading of subtle cues and the authority to commit a party to a decision. Even with AI-assisted preparation, these are not tasks where a model can be left to act on its own.

Paralegals and legal assistants experience a similar split. They still spend a large share of time on document organisation, fact chronology and compliance checklists. Some of those tasks are exposed to AI automation, especially when structured and rule-based. The less structured parts, like communicating with clients under supervision or deciding the internal logical flow of a legal argument, remain human-led.

Judges and hearing officers also offer a useful comparison. Their core work—listening to evidence, weighing arguments, and explaining decisions—remains firmly human. But the research and drafting done by clerks are increasingly exposed to AI. This spread of exposure within a legal occupation is why a single job title does not predict the impact; only a task-level view can.

## Where judgement still wins

AI drafting is not just a time-saver; it changes the relationship to the work. A lawyer reviewing an AI draft acts as a manager, editor and safety net. That requires a deeper understanding of the law, not a shallower one. You need to know what to look for, which question the tool failed to consider, and why a certain phrase could create more liability than it removes.

Judgement also matters outside the text itself. A lawyer must weigh whether to fight a point or settle, decide which precedent the judge will care about, and know when a client’s real problem is not legal at all. No amount of prompt engineering replaces that. The human also carries the professional obligation, confidentiality, and the risk of malpractice. If a model makes an error, the lawyer is still accountable to the court and to the client.

## What this means for legal teams

The first consequence is that legal professionals will use AI for cost-effective volume work. Clients will expect faster drafts and leaner invoices. The second consequence is that people with strong analytical judgement will become more valuable, not less. The bottleneck shifts from producing clear prose to deciding whether the prose is clear, correct, and wise enough to rely on.

Training budgets should therefore be spent on “AI-plus-judgement” skills. New lawyers need to learn how to interrogate model outputs, verify citations, and untangle conflicting standards. Experienced partners need to set guardrails for how AI-generated material is used, ethically and procedurally.

## Takeaway

AI will not stop with drafting, but the spectrum of legal tasks means exposure will remain uneven. Routine language-transformation tasks will increasingly be done by machines, supervised by people. High-judgement advisory and advocacy work will stay human, but will be augmented by AI-prepared material. For policymakers, legal educators, and anyone tracking occupational exposure, the question is no longer “will AI replace lawyers?” but “what do lawyers become when drafting is cheap?” The answer: editors of arguments, stewards of judgement, and the human bridge between a model’s words and a client’s risk.
