## The gap between a working model and a working product

A model that performs well in a notebook is not the same as a system a business can rely on. Between the two sits a long list of unglamorous questions: Which task should the model actually do? What data can it legally touch? Who checks its output? What happens when it is wrong? How is its performance measured once real users arrive?

Most organisations are discovering that the hard part is rarely the model itself. It is the translation work around it. That work has started to harden into recognisable roles.

## What these bridging roles actually do

### AI product managers

These are product managers who understand enough about model behaviour to make honest trade-offs. They decide where probabilistic output is acceptable and where it is not, how to scope a first release that does not overpromise, and how to define success in terms users and finance can both recognise. Their value is judgement about fit, not model architecture.

### Applied AI and solutions engineers

The applied engineer takes a promising prototype and makes it survive contact with production: latency, cost per request, error handling, evaluation harnesses, and integration with systems that were never designed for it. This role borrows heavily from software engineering but adds a working fluency in model evaluation and data pipelines.

### Domain translators and AI-literate specialists

Perhaps the least discussed and most important group: people from the business side who can specify what good looks like. A claims specialist who can describe, in precise terms, how an assessor reaches a decision is often more valuable to an AI project than another engineer. These specialists turn tacit expertise into specifications, test cases and acceptance criteria. Without them, teams optimise for what is easy to measure rather than what matters.

### Governance, evaluation and assurance roles

Someone has to own the questions of accuracy, bias, auditability and regulatory fit. Titles vary — AI risk analyst, model assurance lead, responsible-AI programme manager — but the function is consistent: making the system defensible before and after launch. ### Enablement and change roles

Adoption is its own discipline. The people who design workflows, train colleagues and measure whether a tool is genuinely used — rather than merely licensed — sit squarely in this bridge.

## Why these roles are appearing now

The pattern is familiar from earlier platform shifts. When cloud computing matured, the scarce skill was not writing code but designing systems that were reliable, cost-aware and secure. Generative AI adds a twist: systems are probabilistic, so quality must be measured continuously rather than assumed. That pushes evaluation, monitoring and human review from afterthoughts into job descriptions.

There is also an organisational reason. AI projects fail for reasons that look mundane — unclear ownership, mismatched incentives, data that nobody documented, expectations set by a striking demo. Bridging roles exist to absorb that friction. They are, in effect, the human interface between a statistical system and an institution that runs on accountability.

## How this maps onto AI task exposure

This is where a task-level view is more useful than a headline about "jobs replaced." Our exposure analysis asks which specific tasks within an occupation can be materially assisted or automated by generative AI, and which cannot. The bridging roles score interestingly on both sides.

- **Software developers** show high exposure on discrete tasks such as boilerplate code, test scaffolding and documentation — all of which these new roles lean on heavily. The role persists because integration, judgement and accountability remain firmly human.
- **Project and product managers** see substantial exposure in scheduling, status reporting and drafting, while stakeholder negotiation and prioritisation stay largely untouched. The AI product manager is essentially the old role with a new, harder subject matter.
- **Business and data analysts** face meaningful exposure in routine summarisation and dashboard production, which raises the value of the interpretive and specification work that domain translators do.
- **Compliance and risk specialists** are moderately exposed on documentation review, yet the assurance function is expanding as systems require ongoing scrutiny — a case where automation and demand grow together.
- **Customer service and administrative roles** show high task exposure in drafting and triage, which is precisely why enablement and workflow-design skills matter in those functions.

The consistent finding: exposure concentrates on tasks that produce text, code and analysis from existing information. The bridging roles are built around the tasks that remain stubbornly human — deciding what should be built, judging whether output is acceptable, and owning the consequences.

## What this means for people and employers

For individuals, the practical signal is that domain depth plus AI fluency is a stronger combination than either alone. A specialist who can write clear specifications and evaluate model output is more durable than one who only masters a tool. For employers, the risk is treating these roles as temporary project staff. The organisations getting value from AI appear to be the ones embedding translation and assurance permanently, not staffing them per pilot [VERIFY: comparative adoption evidence].

## Takeaway

The shift from model to product is creating a layer of roles whose core skill is translation: between statistical systems and business accountability, between what a model can do and what an organisation should rely on. Task-exposure data suggests these roles are not a bubble around the technology but the durable human complement to it. The people who can specify, evaluate and own AI-enabled work will remain in demand precisely because the technology is powerful but not accountable — and accountability does not automate.
