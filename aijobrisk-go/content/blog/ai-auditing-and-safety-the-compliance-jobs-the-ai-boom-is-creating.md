## A new layer of work around the model

Every technology that touches money, health or safety eventually attracts a paper trail. Accounting produced auditors; food and drugs produced inspectors; data protection produced privacy officers. Generative AI is now generating its own oversight layer, and it is hiring.

The roles cluster around a few verbs: **audit, evaluate, monitor, document, govern**. AI auditors test whether a model behaves as claimed. Safety researchers probe failure modes. Model risk analysts borrow methods from financial risk and apply them to systems that produce text, images and recommendations. Compliance specialists translate regulatory text into internal controls. Evaluation engineers build the test harnesses that make any of this repeatable.

What makes this wave distinctive is that much of it is *about* AI rather than *done by* AI. The work is judgment-heavy, evidence-heavy and document-heavy — exactly the profile that tends to resist easy automation.

## Why the demand is structural, not a fad

Three pressures keep this work in demand.

First, **regulation is arriving**. The EU AI Act establishes obligations for high-risk systems that include risk management, technical documentation, logging and human oversight. Implementation timing and national enforcement vary, so organisations need jurisdiction-specific legal advice rather than assuming one global timetable.

Second, **procurement**. Enterprises buying AI tools increasingly ask vendors for evidence of testing, bias evaluation and incident handling. A supplier that cannot produce documentation becomes harder to buy from. Compliance becomes commercial.

Third, **incidents**. Models misbehave in public. Each visible failure raises the internal cost of not having someone whose job is to look.

None of this guarantees a permanent boom. Compliance markets can consolidate, and some auditing will be automated or absorbed into existing assurance firms. But the underlying function — independent checking of systems that make consequential decisions — is unlikely to disappear.

## Who actually does this work

The important point, and the one our exposure data keeps surfacing, is that AI safety and auditing is less a single new profession than a **recomposition of existing ones**.

### Adjacent occupations moving sideways

- **Accountants and auditors** already understand evidence, sampling and control testing. Moving from financial statements to model documentation is a genuine stretch, but a shorter one than starting from scratch.
- **Compliance officers** in finance and health already run regimes built on documented controls and audit trails. AI governance is a new chapter in a familiar book.
- **Data protection and privacy officers** have spent years operationalising rules that are vague on purpose. That skill transfers directly.
- **Cybersecurity analysts** already think in adversarial terms, which is most of what red-teaming a model requires.
- **Software testers and QA engineers** understand coverage, regression and reproducibility — the backbone of any evaluation pipeline.

### Genuinely new titles

Some roles are new enough to lack settled definitions: AI auditor, model risk analyst for generative systems, evaluation engineer, AI safety engineer, responsible-AI programme lead. These sit mostly inside consultancies, assurance firms, larger technology companies and, increasingly, regulated sectors such as finance and health.

## The task-exposure lens

Our angle is simple: instead of asking whether a *job* is safe, ask which *tasks* inside it are exposed.

For an AI auditor, the exposed tasks are real and substantial. Drafting documentation, summarising test results, generating first-pass risk registers, writing evaluation scripts, comparing a model's outputs against a policy checklist — all of these are plausibly assisted or partly automated by the very systems being audited. That is an unusual and slightly awkward dynamic: the tool and the oversight of the tool are entangled.

The **less exposed** tasks are the ones that define the role's value. Deciding whether a test is adequate. Judging whether a failure mode matters for a specific deployment. Pushing back on a product team. Signing an opinion that carries liability. Explaining to a regulator why a control is proportionate. These are accountability tasks, and accountability has historically been hard to delegate to software.

So the honest picture is mixed. AI task-exposure analysis suggests compliance and auditing occupations are **moderately exposed but structurally protected** — exposed because so much of the work is text, protected because so much of it is judgment with consequences attached.

Compare that with occupations where documentation is the whole job. Those face sharper exposure. Occupations where the work is physical, relational or site-specific face less. Auditing sits in the middle, and the middle is where the interesting reskilling questions live.

## What this means for people and employers

The bottleneck is not enthusiasm; it is credible competence. A two-day course does not produce someone who can defend an evaluation methodology to a regulator. Apprenticeship-style routes — auditing under supervision, reviewing real systems, seeing how controls fail in practice — look more durable than certificates.

For employers, the practical question is whether AI governance sits in a dedicated team or is embedded in existing assurance functions. Both models exist. The embedded model tends to work better when the surrounding culture already respects independent challenge.

For individuals in adjacent roles, the transferable assets are concrete: evidence discipline, control design, adversarial thinking, and the willingness to write things down precisely. Those are learnable and portable.

## Takeaway

The AI boom is creating compliance, auditing and safety work — but mostly by **extending existing assurance occupations into a new object of scrutiny**, not by inventing a profession from nothing. Task-level exposure suggests these roles will be reshaped by AI tools rather than replaced by them, because the core of the job is judgment under accountability. The people best placed to fill them are those who already know how to test a claim, document a control and stand behind a conclusion.
