## The question is narrower than it looks

"Is AI creating or destroying jobs?" is almost impossible to answer at the level of a whole economy. The more useful question, and the one employers and job-seekers actually face, is which industries are adding headcount because of AI. That framing forces us to look at specific tasks, because AI tends to change the composition of work inside an industry before it changes the total number of jobs.

The honest short answer: a handful of sectors are clearly hiring more, several are hiring differently, and some are quietly shrinking the parts of their workforce tied to routine cognitive tasks. We should be wary of anyone who claims a clean national number for either side.

## Where AI is visibly adding roles

The clearest hiring growth is not in the companies building models. It is in the industries that have to deploy, govern and maintain AI systems inside existing operations.

### Technology and data infrastructure

Demand continues for data engineers, platform engineers, MLOps specialists and security engineers. AI workloads are infrastructure-hungry, so cloud, networking and data-centre operations roles benefit directly. This is genuine net addition in many organisations rather than substitution, because the work (pipelines, access control, monitoring, cost management) did not exist at the same scale a few years ago.

### Professional and business services

Consultancies, systems integrators and specialist advisory firms are hiring to help clients assess, pilot and integrate AI. Much of this is project-based and cyclical, so it is more fragile than it looks, but it is real hiring. Legal and compliance functions inside these firms are also growing as organisations grapple with data protection, model risk and procurement rules.

### Healthcare and life sciences

Administrative and documentation roles are being reshaped, while demand grows for people who can validate, audit and operate clinical and research tooling. Clinical documentation, imaging support, pharmacovigilance and regulatory affairs are areas where AI shifts what the job involves and where specialist oversight is needed. Headcount in health overall is driven more by demographics than by AI, but the AI-specific roles are additive.

### Financial services

The sector is hiring for model risk, fraud analytics, anti-money-laundering operations and data governance. AI increases the volume of models that must be validated and monitored, which creates oversight work. The pattern is uneven across firms, and back-office processing roles are under pressure, so this is a case of internal reallocation more than straightforward growth.

### Education and training

Some of the most durable new demand is in reskilling and instructional design. Organisations need people who can teach staff to work with AI tools safely and productively. This is often an expansion of existing learning-and-development teams rather than a new industry.

## The other side: industries hiring differently or less

It would be misleading to present AI as a pure job engine. Customer support, routine content production, basic translation, some paralegal work and parts of bookkeeping face real substitution pressure. Retail and administrative back offices are also exposed. The distinction that matters is between industries adding roles and industries changing roles: in the second group, overall headcount may be flat while the mix of skills shifts sharply toward supervision, exception handling and client relationship work.

## Mapping this onto AI task-exposure

This is where an occupation-level view helps. Aggregate industry labels hide a lot. What AI actually touches is tasks, and the same industry can contain both highly exposed and highly resilient roles.

Consider a few contrasting occupations. A **data scientist** or **software developer** sits in a field where AI augments core work and where demand has held up, though the tasks inside the job are shifting toward evaluation, systems design and oversight. A **financial analyst** faces meaningful exposure in drafting and routine modelling, while judgement-heavy and client-facing tasks remain relatively protected. A **registered nurse** has high exposure on documentation and administrative tasks but very low exposure on physical care, which is why the role is not being displaced. A **customer service representative** is among the more exposed occupations because a large share of the job is text- and voice-based and rule-bound.

The practical implication is that "which industry is hiring" is the wrong unit of analysis on its own. Two people in the same sector can face very different exposure depending on whether their day is dominated by routine information processing or by physical work, accountability and relationship management.

## What to watch rather than what to believe

Be sceptical of precise forecasts that claim a specific number of net new jobs by a specific year. The evidence base is thin, methods vary widely, and the technology itself is changing faster than the surveys. More reliable signals are job-posting composition, the emergence of genuinely new titles, and wage premiums for oversight and integration skills.

## Takeaway

AI is adding hiring most clearly in the industries that must build, deploy, govern and teach the technology: technology and data infrastructure, professional services, parts of finance and health, and training. Elsewhere it is more likely to change the mix of roles than to expand headcount. For individuals, the safer bet is to look at your own task exposure rather than your industry label, and to move toward the parts of the work that involve judgement, accountability and physical presence.
