The counterintuitive hiring story

The public conversation about generative AI and work tends to assume a single direction: automation arrives, tasks disappear, headcount falls. In two very different parts of the economy — healthcare and the skilled trades — the evidence points somewhere else. In both, AI is being adopted fastest in the administrative and informational layer that surrounds the work, while the physical, relational and judgement-heavy core of the job stays stubbornly human. The result is not mass substitution but a reallocation of time, and in many settings a rise in demand for people.

That is not a claim that these sectors are immune to disruption. It is a claim about where the exposure actually sits. Our own task-exposure data is built on exactly this logic: an occupation is not one task, and the share of its tasks that language models can meaningfully assist is usually far smaller than headlines imply for hands-on roles.

What AI is actually doing in healthcare

In clinical settings, generative AI shows up first in documentation, coding, scheduling, and drafting patient communication. Ambient scribing tools that turn a consultation into a structured note are the clearest example. These tools do not diagnose; they reduce the clerical burden that has long pushed clinicians toward burnout.

When documentation gets cheaper, two things happen. First, existing staff can spend more of their day on direct care. Second, organisations can justify hiring for the roles that were previously squeezed out by admin load — care coordinators, community nurses, allied health staff. Labour shortages in health are driven overwhelmingly by demographics and training pipelines, not by automation, so any relief on the administrative side tends to ease pressure rather than eliminate posts.

The caution is real. Some administrative and transcription roles will shrink, and entry-level back-office work is genuinely exposed. The hiring story is not uniform across the sector; it is concentrated in patient-facing and hands-on roles.

Why the trades are holding firm

Skilled trades — electricians, plumbers, HVAC technicians, welders, construction supervisors — sit at the opposite end of the exposure spectrum. Language models can help write a quote, summarise a safety regulation, or draft a maintenance schedule. They cannot run a conduit, diagnose a fault by smell and sound, or make a judgement call in a cramped ceiling void.

The adoption pattern in trades mirrors healthcare: AI eats the paperwork around the job. Estimating, compliance documentation, permit drafting, and customer communication are all being partly automated. For small firms, that is a capacity unlock — the owner who spent evenings on admin can take on more work, which in turn supports hiring an apprentice or a second crew member.

There is also a demand-side effect. Data-centre buildouts, grid upgrades, heat-pump retrofits, and electrification all require more physical installation and maintenance labour. AI is a driver of that demand as much as a substitute for any particular task.

The exposure map: where the line falls

This is where task-level analysis matters more than occupation-level labels. Consider the contrast:

Roles with high exposure in their administrative share

  • Registered nurses: documentation, care planning, and patient messaging are meaningfully assistable; bedside assessment, procedures, and advocacy are not.
  • Medical coders and health information staff: a large share of the role is structured text transformation, which is precisely what generative models handle well.
  • Construction estimators: takeoffs, quote drafting, and supplier correspondence are exposed; site assessment and negotiation are not.

Roles with low exposure overall

  • Electricians and plumbers: the task mix is dominated by physical manipulation, fault-finding in situ, and safety-critical judgement.
  • Care workers and nursing assistants: relational and physical care tasks remain firmly human.
  • Welders and sheet-metal workers: precision physical work with limited text component.

Teasing out these shares — rather than asking whether a whole occupation is "automatable" — is the point of an exposure index. Jobs are bundles, and the bundle composition determines whether AI reads as a threat, a tool, or a recruitment advert.

The reskilling signal, not the redundancy signal

Across both sectors, the practical response is the same: train people to use AI for the administrative layer while deepening the hands-on and relational skills that remain the core of the job. Health systems that invest in AI-assisted documentation and redeploy the saved hours into patient contact tend to report better retention. Trade employers that automate estimating and compliance free up senior staff to supervise more apprentices.

The risks worth watching are not robot doctors or robot plumbers. They are narrower: back-office roles that quietly disappear, and a widening gap between firms that adopt productivity tools and those that do not. Both are policy questions about training and access, not signs of a sector in decline.

Takeaway

Healthcare and the trades are not counter-examples to AI disruption; they are illustrations of how task composition drives the outcome. Where AI touches the paperwork around a job, it tends to increase the value of the human hours that remain — and that frequently means more hiring, not less. The right question for workers, employers, and policymakers is not "will AI take this job?" but "which tasks in this job will AI absorb, and what should people do with the time that frees up?" Answer that honestly, and the hiring case for hands-on work becomes much clearer.
