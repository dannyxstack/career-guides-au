# Journalist — career analysis · Iceland

**Occupation code: 2642 (ISCO08)**

Journalists research, write, and report news for print, broadcast, and online media. In Iceland, the profession is concentrated in Reykjavík, with a strong tradition of press freedom and a small, close-knit media industry. Many journalists work as freelancers or for multiple outlets. Digital transformation has shifted focus to multimedia storytelling, with demand for skills in video, podcasting, and social media. Fluency in Icelandic is essential, as most media is in Icelandic. Entry typically requires a university degree in journalism or related field, and experience with local news is highly valued.

## 3. Job demand / competition / intensity

**Job demand: medium (★★½☆☆).** National workforce approx. 330. Employment outlook for journalists in Iceland is stable but competitive. Traditional print media is declining, while online and multimedia outlets are growing. Demand exists for journalists who can produce content across platforms, including digital video, podcasts, and data journalism. Freelance opportunities are common, but job security can be limited. The market is small, so networking and a strong portfolio are crucial. Overall, prospects are best for those with specialized knowledge (e.g., investigative or business journalism) and digital skills.

**Competition: medium-high (★★★½☆).**

## 5. Future outlook / AI exposure

**Future prospect: medium (★★½☆☆).**
**AI replacement risk: medium-high (★★★½☆).**

Career progression typically starts as a junior reporter or news assistant, advancing to senior correspondent, editor, or specialized beat reporter. Many journalists gain experience at regional media before moving to national outlets in Reykjavík. Freelancing is a common path, allowing for portfolio building and networking. Advancement often requires a track record of compelling stories and leadership. Some journalists transition to communications, public relations, or academia. Continuous learning in digital tools and multimedia production is essential for career growth.

**Tasks most exposed to AI:**

- Automatically generate standard news briefs (e.g., financial reports, sports scores)
- Speech-to-text and automatic subtitle generation
- Fact-checking and data reconciliation (e.g., verifying statistical data)

**Where AI augments the role:**

- AI-assisted data analysis and visualization (quickly identify trends)
- AI-recommended topics and audience preference prediction
- AI compiles multilingual information summaries

**Human moat:**

- Exclusive interviews and in-depth investigations (interpersonal insight and trust)
- Ethical judgment and sensitive issue gatekeeping
- Creative storytelling (features, commentary)

**Skills to build:**

- Data journalism (Python/R/data visualization)
- Multimedia production (video/podcast editing)
- AI tool applications (e.g., ChatGPT-assisted writing)
- Fact-checking and information verification
- Audience Analysis and Digital Marketing

**Key growth areas:**

- Digital media
- Multimedia journalism
- Investigative reporting
- Podcast production

## 8. FAQ

**Q: What is the average salary for a journalist in Iceland?**
A: Salaries vary by experience, medium, and location. According to Statistics Iceland, the average monthly salary for journalists is competitive with other professions requiring a university degree. Entry-level positions may start lower, while experienced journalists at national media earn above average. Freelance income can be irregular.

**Q: How can a non-EEA journalist obtain a work permit for Iceland?**
A: Non-EEA journalists must first secure a job offer from an Icelandic employer. The employer then applies for a labour market test to show no suitable EEA/EFTA candidate is available. Once approved, the journalist applies for a work and residence permit at the Directorate of Immigration. The permit is tied to the specific job.

**Q: Do I need to speak Icelandic to work as a journalist in Iceland?**
A: Yes, virtually all media in Iceland operate in Icelandic, and fluency is required for reporting, editing, and interviewing. Exceptions may exist for international outlets but are rare.
