# Inland and Coastal Waters Fishery Worker — career analysis · Iceland

**Occupation code: 6222 (ISCO08)**

Inland and coastal waters fishery workers in Iceland engage in fishing and aquaculture activities in lakes, rivers, and coastal areas. This occupation includes netting, trapping, angling, and fish farming. While Iceland's commercial fishing industry is dominated by deep-sea vessels, the inland and coastal sector serves local markets, tourism (angling), and emerging aquaculture operations. Workers often operate small boats or work on fish farms, facing seasonal demand and strict quota regulations. The role requires physical stamina, adaptability to harsh weather, and knowledge of sustainable practices.

## 5. Future outlook / AI exposure

**Future prospect: low (★☆☆☆☆).**
**AI replacement risk: low (★☆☆☆☆).**

Career progression typically starts as a deckhand or farm assistant. With experience and certifications (e.g., skipper's license, fish farming diplomas), workers can advance to boat captain or aquaculture facility manager. Some move into fisheries inspection, resource management, or tourism guiding. The path is narrow but rewarding for those committed to the sector.

**Tasks most exposed to AI:**

- Manual net casting and retrieval operations: automatic nets and robotic arms can handle this
- Manual sorting and weighing of catch: machine vision and automatic grading systems
- Manual navigation and obstacle avoidance for fishing vessels: GPS and autopilot systems
- Visual monitoring of fishery resources: drones and remote sensing technology
- Hand repair of fishing gear: automatic repair equipment

**Where AI augments the role:**

- Using drone and satellite data to identify fish locations, improving fishing efficiency
- Real-time monitoring of environmental parameters such as water quality and temperature via IoT sensors
- Use data analysis to optimize navigation routes and working hours
- Using machine vision to assist in catch quality grading and inventory management
- Using digital platforms for sustainable fishery certification and traceability

**Human moat:**

- Ability to repair and make emergency decisions for sudden mechanical failures
- Safe judgment and operations under complex weather and sea conditions
- Local ecological knowledge and traditional fishing experience
- Compliance communication with regulatory authorities and environmental organizations
- Manual processing of high-value catch (e.g., rare fish)

**Skills to build:**

- Automated fishing gear and drone inspection operations
- Fisheries Data Collection and Analysis (Python/R)
- Applications of GIS and remote sensing technology in fisheries
- Fisheries machinery repair and basic electrical skills
- Knowledge of sustainable fisheries management and regulations
- Aquaculture and Resource Monitoring Technology

**Key growth areas:**

- Aquaculture
- Sustainable fishing
- Rivers and lakes management
- Tourism fishing

## 8. FAQ

**Q: What is the typical salary for this occupation?**
A: Earnings are modest and often seasonal, typically below the national average. Entry-level workers may earn around minimum wage, while experienced skippers or farm managers can earn more. Income varies significantly with catch and season.

**Q: Can non-EEA nationals obtain a work permit as a fishery worker?**
A: Generally, no. The occupation is classified as low-skilled and fails the labour market test unless the employer proves no EEA worker is available. Even then, the wage threshold for qualified professionals is rarely met.

**Q: Is there room for advancement in this field?**
A: Yes, through gaining experience and certifications such as a skipper's license or aquaculture management diploma. Progression to boat captain, farm supervisor, or self-employment is possible.
