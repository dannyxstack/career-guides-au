# Nursing Professional — career analysis · Iceland

**Occupation code: 2221 (ISCO08)**

Nursing professionals in Iceland provide comprehensive patient care in hospitals, primary health centres, and long-term care facilities. They assess health needs, administer treatments, and coordinate care plans. The profession is highly regulated, requiring a BSc in Nursing and registration with the Directorate of Health. Due to an ageing population and a chronic shortage of nurses, demand is consistently strong, especially in rural areas and specialised fields like intensive care and geriatrics. Icelandic employers actively recruit from abroad, but language proficiency in Icelandic is typically required for direct patient interaction.

## 3. Job demand / competition / intensity

**Job demand: very high (★★★★½).** National workforce approx. 1,480. The employment outlook for nursing professionals in Iceland is very positive. A growing elderly population and the expansion of healthcare services drive sustained demand. The government has designated nursing as a shortage occupation, leading to targeted recruitment campaigns and streamlined visa processes. However, the supply of new graduates cannot keep pace, opening opportunities for qualified foreign nurses. Telehealth and home care are emerging sectors. Despite occasional budget constraints, job security is high, and salaries are competitive within the public sector.

**Competition: medium (★★½☆☆).**

## 5. Future outlook / AI exposure

**Future prospect: high (★★★★☆).**
**AI replacement risk: medium (★★½☆☆).**

Career progression for nurses typically moves from clinical roles to specialist practice, management, or education. Nurses can advance to charge nurse, nurse manager, or clinical nurse specialist after gaining experience and postgraduate qualifications. Many pursue master's degrees to become nurse practitioners or educators. The Directorate of Health offers continuing education programmes. With increasing complexity of care, opportunities in research and policy development are growing. Mobility between public and private sectors is common, and there is a clear path to head nurse or director of nursing in larger institutions.

## 8. FAQ

**Q: What is the typical salary range for nurses in Iceland?**
A: Salaries for nursing professionals in Iceland are competitive, with entry-level positions around 4,000,000 ISK annually and experienced specialists earning up to 7,000,000 ISK or more. Compensation includes benefits such as pension contributions, overtime pay, and shift differentials. Public sector wages are set by collective bargaining agreements.

**Q: Do I need a visa to work as a nurse in Iceland if I am from outside the EEA?**
A: Yes, non-EEA nurses require a work permit based on a job offer and a labour market test. You must first secure registration with the Directorate of Health, then apply for a permit through the Directorate of Immigration. The process can take 1-3 months, and the permit is initially tied to a specific employer.

**Q: Is Icelandic language proficiency mandatory for nursing?**
A: Yes, for most nursing roles, especially those involving direct patient care, Icelandic proficiency at about B2 level is required for registration. However, in some specialist roles or research positions, English may suffice temporarily. Most employers offer language courses.
