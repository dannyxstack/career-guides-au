# Subsistence Fisher, Hunter, Trapper or Gatherer — career analysis · China

**Occupation code: 6340 (ISCO08)**

Subsistence fishers, hunters, trappers and gatherers in China engage in traditional harvesting of wild aquatic animals, game, fish, and natural plant resources primarily for personal or community consumption. These activities are concentrated in remote rural and ethnic minority regions such as Yunnan, Tibet, Inner Mongolia, and parts of the northeast, with limited commercial value. The occupation is characterized by declining participation due to urbanization, environmental protection policies, and restrictions on hunting and trapping. Most practitioners are older and rely on indigenous knowledge. Chinese labor statistics do not classify this as a distinct formal occupation; it falls under broader categories of agricultural or forestry workers.

## 3. Job demand / competition / intensity

National policies emphasizing ecological restoration, bans on wildlife hunting (due to disease control and conservation), and fishing moratoriums on major rivers (e.g., Yangtze River ten-year fishing ban) are shrinking opportunities. Younger generations are migrating to cities, leaving an aging workforce. Alternative livelihoods such as ecotourism, fish farming, and protected area ranger positions may absorb some, but overall employment is expected to decline. ## 5. Future outlook / AI exposure

**Future prospect: very low (½☆☆☆☆).**

Career progression is nearly nonexistent—there is no formal hierarchy or promotion path. Practitioners typically learn skills from family or community elders. Modernization and legal restrictions are forcing many to transition to related fields like aquaculture, wildlife management, or tourism guiding. Some may obtain permits for limited commercial fishing or licensed trapping, but formal certifications are rare. The occupation is not recognized as a skilled trade in China's vocational system, so training programs are scarce. Individuals seeking stability or income growth are advised to acquire skills in sustainable resource management or switch to regulated industries.

## 6. Who is / isn't suited

**Who is suited**

## 7. FAQ

**Q: What is the typical income for subsistence fishers, hunters, trappers and gatherers in China?**
A: Income is highly variable and generally very low—often below the poverty line (annual under 10,000 CNY). Many rely on barter or supplemental government subsidies. Commercial sale of catch is increasingly restricted. Official salary data for this occupation is not collected due to its informal nature.

**Q: Can a foreigner get a Chinese work visa as a subsistence fisher or hunter?**
A: Extremely unlikely. China's work visa system targets skilled professionals and high-level talent. Subsistence fishing, hunting, trapping, and gathering are not considered skilled occupations, and no shortage lists include them. Local labor supply is abundant. The only theoretical route would be through employer sponsorship for a related role (e.g., ecotourism guide with fishing experience), but practical chances are near zero.

**Q: Are there any formal training programs for this occupation in China?**
A: No formal state-recognized training for subsistence gathering exists. Related vocational training may cover aquaculture, wildlife management, or forestry, but direct skills are passed down informally. The Chinese government promotes vocational education in agriculture (e.g., modern farming) but not traditional subsistence practices.
