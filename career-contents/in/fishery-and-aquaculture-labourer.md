# Fishery and Aquaculture Labourer — career analysis · India

**Occupation code: 9216 (NCO2015)**

Fishery and aquaculture labourers perform manual tasks in fish farms, hatcheries, and fishing operations, including feeding, netting, cleaning ponds, sorting catch, and maintaining equipment. This occupation is common in coastal and inland regions of India such as Kerala, Andhra Pradesh, and West Bengal, often in informal or cooperative settings. Work is seasonal and physically demanding, with limited formal training requirements and low entry barriers.

## 3. Job demand / competition / intensity

**Job demand: medium-high (★★★☆☆).** National workforce approx. 483,606. India's fisheries sector is expanding due to government initiatives like Pradhan Mantri Matsya Sampada Yojana, aiming to double fisherfolk incomes. Employment is projected to grow moderately, especially in aquaculture and inland fisheries. However, mechanisation and climate change may suppress labour demand. Most labourers remain in informal employment without social security, limiting upward mobility.

## 5. Future outlook / AI exposure

**Future prospect: low (★½☆☆☆).**
**AI replacement risk: low (★☆☆☆☆).**

Career progression is minimal; labourers may advance to crew leader or farm supervisor after years of experience, but formal education or certification can accelerate growth. Some move into fish processing, trading, or own small aquaculture ventures. Government training programs offer skilling in modern fishing techniques, but the path remains unstructured.

**Tasks most exposed to AI:**

- Automated feeding systems take over scheduled and quantitative feeding tasks
- Water quality sensors automatically monitor and record parameters like pH and dissolved oxygen.
- Drones or underwater robots automatically inspect fish cages, replacing manual visual inspection
- AI video analysis automatically counts fish schools and detects diseases or abnormal behavior
- Environmental control systems (e.g., aerators) automatically start/stop based on sensor data

**Where AI augments the role:**

- AI-assisted water quality data analysis to predict algal blooms or disease outbreaks, guiding preventive measures
- Automated feeding systems optimize feeding strategies based on fish activity data to improve feed conversion rates
- Digital twin models simulate different breeding scenarios to assist production planning
- Wearable devices monitor worker health to prevent heatstroke or fatigue
- AI document assistant automatically generates farming logs and compliance reports

**Human moat:**

- On-site response capability for sudden equipment failures, extreme weather, and other abnormal conditions
- Manual experience-based judgement of fish and shrimp behaviour and health (e.g., abnormal swimming, colour changes)
- Interpersonal and negotiation skills in coordinating with suppliers, veterinarians, and regulatory bodies
- On-site safety judgment and emergency rescue (e.g., drowning, electric shock)

**Skills to build:**

- Learn to use mainstream smart aquaculture platforms (e.g., AquaManager, eFishery)
- Basic data processing and analysis skills (Excel or simple BI tools)
- Basic skills in operating and maintaining automatic feeding systems, drones, and ROVs.
- Understand working principles of aquaculture Internet of Things (IoT) sensors
- Sustainable farming principles (e.g., antibiotic reduction, carbon footprint accounting)
- English communication skills to suit international companies or export requirements

**Key growth areas:**

- Aquaculture expansion
- Export demand
- Shrimp farming
- Inland fisheries

## 7. FAQ

**Q: What is the average salary for a fishery labourer in India?**
A: Average annual salary ranges from INR 96,000 to INR 300,000 depending on experience and region, with daily wages of INR 300-800. Income is seasonal and insecure.

**Q: Can a fishery labourer migrate to work abroad?**
A: Generally no. This low-skilled occupation does not qualify for skilled migration visas. Some Gulf countries may hire Indian fishermen on temporary work permits, but this is rare and informal.
