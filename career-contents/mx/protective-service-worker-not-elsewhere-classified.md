# Protective Services Worker — career analysis · Mexico

**Occupation code: 5419 (ISCO08)**

Protective Services Workers Not Elsewhere Classified encompass a variety of roles including security guards, bodyguards, private investigators, and surveillance officers who safeguard people, property, and information. They work in diverse settings such as corporate offices, residential complexes, retail stores, hotels, and public events. Duties may include patrolling premises, monitoring security systems, controlling access, conducting inspections, and responding to emergencies. In Mexico, this occupation is predominantly employed in the private security industry, with many workers in informal arrangements. Training typically includes on-the-job learning and voluntary certification, while legal registration with state authorities is mandatory for private security personnel.

## 5. Future outlook / AI exposure

**AI replacement risk: low (★☆☆☆☆).**

Career progression typically starts as an entry-level security guard or patrol officer, with advancement to supervisor, shift manager, or security coordinator. Experienced workers may transition into private investigation, executive protection, or security consulting. Additional training in areas like cybersecurity, risk assessment, or emergency response can open doors to corporate or government roles. However, without further education, the path is often limited to supervisory positions within the same sector. Some workers move into law enforcement or military careers after gaining experience.

## 7. FAQ

**Q: What is the typical salary for a protective services worker in Mexico?**
A: Salaries vary widely by sector and region, but generally range from the minimum wage to about 12,000 MXN per month for entry-level positions. Official statistics from INEGI/ENOE provide more precise figures. Supervisors and specialized roles can earn higher incomes.

**Q: Can a foreigner work as a protective services worker in Mexico under a work visa?**
A: Yes, but it is challenging. The occupation is not considered a skilled migration priority, so employers must prove no local worker is available. Foreign workers typically need a job offer and an employer-sponsored Temporary Resident Visa with a work permit. The process is easier for those with specialized skills (e.g., cybersecurity in security systems) or who are hired by international firms.
