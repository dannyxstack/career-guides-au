# Specialist Medical Practitioner — career analysis · Brazil

**Occupation code: 2212 (ISCO08)**

Specialist medical practitioners in Brazil are highly skilled physicians who have completed medical school and a residency program in a specific field such as cardiology, oncology, or orthopedics. They diagnose and treat complex medical conditions, perform surgeries, and provide specialized care. The profession requires extensive education, including a six-year medical degree and three to five years of residency, plus mandatory registration with the Regional Council of Medicine (CRM). Specialist doctors work in hospitals, private clinics, and public health units, often in high-stress environments with long hours. Demand remains strong due to Brazil's aging population and healthcare system needs, particularly in underserved regions and niche specialties.

## 2. Qualifications & licensing

| Qualification | Issuer | Required | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| CRM Registration | Regional Council of Medicine (CRM) | Required | Mandatory registration to practice medicine in Brazil. Requires recognition of foreign degree or completion of local medical school. |
| Revalidation (Revalida) | INEP / MEC | Required | Required for foreign medical graduates (including from Mercosur countries) to validate their degree. Consists of theoretical and practical exams. |
| Specialty Title | Brazilian Medical Association (AMB) / Ministry of Education (MEC) | Optional | Board certification in a specialty obtained after residency and examination. Valued but not legally mandatory for practice; however, most employers require it. |

## 3. Job demand / competition / intensity

**Job demand: very high (★★★★★).** National workforce approx. 389,730. Brazil's healthcare sector faces a growing demand for specialist medical practitioners, driven by an aging population, rising prevalence of chronic diseases, and expansion of private healthcare. The public SUS system struggles with shortages, especially in rural and remote areas, while private hospitals compete for top talent. The COVID-19 pandemic highlighted gaps in critical care and infectious disease specialties. Government initiatives to boost medical residency slots and telemedicine adoption are creating new opportunities. However, emigration of doctors to higher-paying countries and bureaucratic hurdles for foreign-qualified physicians may temper supply growth. Overall, employment prospects are very favorable for board-certified specialists.

**Competition: medium-high (★★★½☆).**
**Work intensity: very high (★★★★½).**

## 5. Future outlook / AI exposure

**Future prospect: very high (★★★★½).**
**AI replacement risk: low (★☆☆☆☆).**

The typical career path begins with a medical degree (6 years), followed by a residency (3-5 years) in a chosen specialty. New specialists often work in public hospitals or group practices to build experience. Progression includes obtaining additional subspecialty fellowships, academic appointments at universities, or leadership roles in hospital departments. Some open private practices or join large healthcare networks. Career advancement is tied to clinical reputation, research output, and institutional affiliations. The rise of telemedicine and medical tourism is opening new avenues. Persistent demand for subspecialists (e.g., oncology, cardiology) offers strong earning potential and job stability, though competition for top positions in affluent regions remains intense.

## 6. Who is / isn't suited

**Who is suited**
- Individuals with strong academic aptitude and commitment to long, rigorous training.
- Those who thrive in high-pressure, high-responsibility environments and want to make a direct impact on patient health.
- Candidates willing to relocate to underserved regions for better job opportunities.

## 7. FAQ

**Q: What is the typical salary range for a specialist medical practitioner in Brazil?**
A: Salaries vary widely by specialty, location, and sector. A typical specialist in private practice earns between BRL 200,000 and BRL 500,000 annually. Public sector salaries are lower (BRL 120,000–250,000) but with job stability. Subspecialists in high-demand fields (e.g., neurosurgery) can exceed BRL 600,000.

**Q: What is the most common visa route for foreign specialists wanting to work in Brazil?**
A: The VITEM V (work visa) is the primary route, requiring a formal job offer and employer sponsorship. The company must demonstrate that no qualified Brazilian is available. For Mercosur nationals, the Mercosur Residence Agreement offers a simpler path with fewer requirements. All foreign physicians must pass the Revalida exam to have their degree recognized.

**Q: How long does it take to become a specialist in Brazil?**
A: From start of medical school, a specialist typically requires 11–13 years: 6 years of medical school, 1–2 years of general internship, and 3–5 years of residency. Additional fellowships add 1–3 years. Foreign doctors also need time for Revalida and CRM registration.
