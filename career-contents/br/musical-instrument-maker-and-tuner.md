# Musical Instrument Makers and Tuners — career analysis · Brazil

**Occupation code: 7312 (ISCO08)**

Musical Instrument Makers and Tuners craft, repair, and tune a variety of instruments, from violins and guitars to pianos and brass. In Brazil, this niche occupation is anchored in traditional craftsmanship, with many practitioners working in small workshops, music stores, or as self-employed artisans. The role demands manual dexterity, an ear for pitch, and knowledge of woodworking and acoustics. While not a high-volume profession, it serves a steady market of musicians and institutions, particularly in regions with strong musical culture like Rio de Janeiro and Minas Gerais. Training typically occurs through vocational courses or apprenticeships, and formal qualifications are not mandatory but strongly enhance credibility.

## 3. Job demand / competition / intensity

**Job demand: low (★☆☆☆☆).** National workforce approx. 3,051. Brazil's employment outlook for musical instrument makers and tuners is stable but limited. The occupation is tied to the cultural sector, which sees moderate demand from orchestras, music schools, and independent musicians. Growth is constrained by the high cost of premium instruments and a trend toward mass-produced imports. However, niche opportunities exist in restoration of antique instruments and custom building for professional players. The rise of guitar and other instrument sales during the pandemic has increased repair needs. Overall, the market is small and competitive, with few formal positions. Most work is freelance or small-business based, and economic downturns reduce discretionary spending on repairs and custom builds.

**Competition: medium-high (★★★★☆).**
**Work intensity: medium (★★½☆☆).**

## 5. Future outlook / AI exposure

**Future prospect: medium (★★★☆☆).**
**AI replacement risk: low (★☆☆☆☆).**

Career progression typically starts with an apprenticeship or a technical course, followed by years of specialization in a specific instrument type (e.g., lutherie, piano tuning). Experienced makers can advance to workshop managers, open their own ateliers, or teach at vocational schools. Some move into sales or restoration of high-value instruments. International certification, such as from the Piano Technicians Guild or the Guild of American Luthiers, can boost reputation but is not common in Brazil. The path is heavily skill-dependent; those with a reputation for quality can command premium fees. There is little formal hierarchy, and success relies on networking and word-of-mouth.

## 7. FAQ

**Q: What is a typical salary for a musical instrument maker or tuner in Brazil?**
A: Salaries vary widely by instrument and experience. A beginner tuner or repairer may earn R$1,500–2,500 per month (R$18,000–30,000/year), while established luthiers or piano technicians can reach R$4,000–8,000/month (R$48,000–96,000/year). High-end custom makers may charge more per instrument but have inconsistent income.

**Q: Can a foreign musical instrument maker get a work visa for Brazil?**
A: Yes, but it is not easy. The VITEM V visa requires an employer to sponsor you, proving that no qualified Brazilian is available. For self-employed work, the Mercosur Residence Agreement is an option for nationals of member countries. Otherwise, you may need to obtain a CNIg professional authorization, which is discretionary.

**Q: Is it necessary to have formal education to work in this field in Brazil?**
A: No, formal education is not mandatory. Many professionals learn through informal apprenticeships. However, certification from recognized courses (e.g., lutherie, piano tuning) improves credibility and employability, especially for formal positions in music stores or orchestras.
