# Subsistence Crop Farmer — career analysis · Brazil

**Occupation code: 6310 (ISCO08)**

Subsistence crop farmers cultivate small plots of land primarily to feed themselves and their families, with little or no surplus for sale. Common in rural Brazil, especially in the Northeast and North regions, they grow staple crops such as cassava, beans, maize, and rice. Work is typically family-based, using manual tools or rudimentary equipment. Income is unstable and below the minimum wage, and farmers often lack formal land titles or access to credit.

Employment prospects for subsistence farmers are declining due to rural-urban migration, land concentration, and mechanization of agriculture. Government programs like Bolsa Família and technical assistance from Embrapa may offer some support, but overall demand for this occupation is shrinking. Climate change and water scarcity further threaten yields. Job opportunities are limited to remote areas with poor market integration.

## 5. Future outlook / AI exposure

**Future prospect: low (★☆☆☆☆).**
**AI replacement risk: very low (½☆☆☆☆).**

Career progression is extremely limited. Most subsistence farmers remain in the same role for life, passing land to children. A few may transition to small commercial farming if they obtain credit, irrigation, or market access. Others leave for informal urban jobs or migrate to other regions. Formal training or certification is rare; knowledge is transferred orally. The future of this occupation depends on agrarian reform and rural development policies.

**Tasks most exposed to AI:**

- Manual recording of crop growth logs and yield data, replaced by automatic sensors
- Manual pest and disease identification replaced by AI image recognition
- Manual calculation of fertilizer amounts replaced by precision agriculture algorithms
- Paper-based bookkeeping and tax calculations are being replaced by financial software

**Where AI augments the role:**

- AI tools analyze soil moisture and meteorological data to optimize irrigation timing.
- Use satellite imagery and drones to monitor crop health and provide early warning of pests and diseases
- AI-assisted planting decisions based on historical data and market price forecasts
- Online platforms connecting local consumers with restaurants to expand direct sales channels
- Smart devices monitor storage conditions to reduce post-harvest loss

**Human moat:**

- Physical labour and on-site work (e.g., manual weeding, harvesting)
- Decisions based on local experience (e.g., traditional crop rotation, variety selection)
- Trust relationship with communities and consumers
- Flexible judgment in response to sudden weather and pests

**Skills to build:**

- Use AI agricultural applications (e.g., weather prediction, soil analysis)
- Operate smartphones and related agricultural software
- Basic data analysis and digital record-keeping
- Marketing and social media promotion of local products
- Learn fundamentals of precision agriculture
- Sustainable agriculture practices (e.g., organic certification, water-saving technologies)

**Key growth areas:**

- Subsistence
- Family farming
- Rural exodus
- Agroecology

## 6. Who is / isn't suited

**Who is suited**

- People who accept physical labor and low, irregular income.
- Those seeking self-sufficiency and minimal dependence on markets.

**Who is not suited**

## 7. FAQ

**Q: How much do subsistence crop farmers earn in Brazil?**
A: Income is highly variable and often non-monetary; average monthly income is below the minimum wage (around R$200–R$600 from occasional sales). Most production is for self-consumption.

**Q: Can a foreigner obtain a visa to work as a subsistence farmer in Brazil?**
A: It is very difficult. The main route is the Mercosur Residence Agreement, allowing citizens of neighboring countries to live and work in Brazil. Otherwise, a work visa would require employer sponsorship, which is rare for subsistence farming.

**Q: What is the typical career path for a subsistence farmer?**
A: Most stay in the same activity their whole lives. Some may transition to small commercial farming through extension programs, but opportunities are limited. Many younger people leave for urban jobs.
