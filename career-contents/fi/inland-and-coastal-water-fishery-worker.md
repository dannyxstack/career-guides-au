# Inland and Coastal Waters Fishery Worker — career analysis · Finland

**Occupation code: 6222 (ISCO08)**

This occupation covers workers who catch fish and other aquatic organisms in inland waters and coastal areas. In Finland, the sector is small-scale, often family-run, and faces challenges from declining fish stocks, EU quotas, and climate change. Employment is seasonal and concentrated in the archipelago, Lake District, and Baltic coast. Mechanization and aquaculture have reduced demand, but sustainable fishing and tourism-linked angling offer niche opportunities. The workforce is aging, creating replacement needs, but entry barriers include high costs for equipment and licenses. Most workers are self-employed or crew on small boats.

## 5. Future outlook / AI exposure

**Future prospect: low (★☆☆☆☆).**
**AI replacement risk: low (★☆☆☆☆).**

Career progression is minimal; workers typically remain as self-employed fishers or crew. Some move into aquaculture, fish processing, or fisheries management. Vocational training can enhance skills but is not mandatory. Many fishers diversify into tourism, guiding fishing trips, or selling directly to consumers. The profession is often passed down through families. With declining catches, some leave the industry entirely. Networking and local knowledge are key to survival.

**Tasks most exposed to AI:**

- Manual net casting and retrieval operations: automatic nets and robotic arms can handle this
- Manual sorting and weighing of catch: machine vision and automatic grading systems
- Manual navigation and obstacle avoidance for fishing vessels: GPS and autopilot systems
- Visual monitoring of fishery resources: drones and remote sensing technology
- Hand repair of fishing gear: automatic repair equipment

**Where AI augments the role:**

- Using drone and satellite data to identify fish locations, improving fishing efficiency
- Real-time monitoring of environmental parameters such as water quality and temperature via IoT sensors
- Use data analysis to optimize navigation routes and working hours
- Using machine vision to assist in catch quality grading and inventory management
- Using digital platforms for sustainable fishery certification and traceability

**Human moat:**

- Ability to repair and make emergency decisions for sudden mechanical failures
- Safe judgment and operations under complex weather and sea conditions
- Local ecological knowledge and traditional fishing experience
- Compliance communication with regulatory authorities and environmental organizations
- Manual processing of high-value catch (e.g., rare fish)

**Skills to build:**

- Automated fishing gear and drone inspection operations
- Fisheries Data Collection and Analysis (Python/R)
- Applications of GIS and remote sensing technology in fisheries
- Fisheries machinery repair and basic electrical skills
- Knowledge of sustainable fisheries management and regulations
- Aquaculture and Resource Monitoring Technology

**Key growth areas:**

- sustainable fishing
- tourism-linked fishing
- local food
- aquaculture integration

## 8. FAQ

**Q: What is the typical salary for inland and coastal waters fishery workers in Finland?**
A: Salaries vary greatly by season and catch. Pre-tax annual earnings are typically between €20,000 and €30,000, but many self-employed fishers earn less. Income is directly tied to catches and market prices.

**Q: Can a non-EU citizen get a work permit as a fishery worker in Finland?**
A: It is difficult. The job must meet the TTOL permit requirements, including a salary threshold (approx. €1,270/month in 2025) and labor market testing. Given low wages and seasonal nature, few permits are granted. EU/EEA citizens have an easier route.

**Q: Is formal training required to become a fishery worker in Finland?**
A: No formal training is mandatory, but a vocational qualification in fishing (ammattitutkinto) is recommended. A commercial fishing license from the ELY Centre is required. Courses are available at vocational schools.
