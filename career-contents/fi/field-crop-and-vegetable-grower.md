# Field Crop and Vegetable Grower — career analysis · Finland

**Occupation code: 6111 (ISCO08)**

Field crop and vegetable growers in Finland manage agricultural land to produce cereals, vegetables, berries, and other crops. They work on farms, often in rural areas, and tasks include soil preparation, planting, fertilizing, irrigation, pest control, harvesting, and post-harvest handling. The sector is characterized by a mix of small family farms and large commercial operations, with increasing emphasis on organic production and sustainable practices. Growers must adapt to Finland's short growing season and cold climate, often utilizing greenhouses and polytunnels to extend production. The occupation requires practical skills in farming machinery, crop management, and business planning, as many growers are self-employed farm owners.

## 3. Job demand / competition / intensity

**Job demand: high (★★★★☆).** National workforce approx. 12,088. Employment outlook for field crop and vegetable growers in Finland is stable with moderate demand driven by domestic food production and export markets. The aging farmer population presents opportunities for new entrants, but land consolidation and mechanization reduce the number of jobs. Growth areas include organic farming, greenhouse vegetables, and local food systems. Climate change may alter growing conditions, creating both challenges and opportunities for adaptation. The sector relies heavily on seasonal labour, and the COVID-19 pandemic highlighted vulnerabilities in supply chains, prompting increased interest in self-sufficiency. Overall, job openings are expected to be limited to replacement demand and niche specializations.

## 5. Future outlook / AI exposure

**Future prospect: medium-high (★★★☆☆).**
**AI replacement risk: medium-low (★★☆☆☆).**

Career progression typically starts as a farm worker or trainee, advancing to farm manager or self-employed grower. Experienced growers may specialize in high-value crops (e.g., organic berries, greenhouse tomatoes) or diversify into agritourism, direct sales, or processing. Some pursue higher education in agronomy or agricultural engineering for research or advisory roles. Farm ownership is common, but requires significant capital investment. Technology adoption (precision agriculture, automation) creates opportunities for tech-savvy growers. Cooperative models and contract farming provide pathways for those without land. The ageing farmer population means succession planning is critical, and younger growers can benefit from mentoring programs and startup subsidies.

**Tasks most exposed to AI:**

- Using traditional tractors for repetitive field operations such as sowing and tillage
- Apply fertilizers and pesticides manually or on a fixed schedule
- Visually inspecting crop health and pests
- Manually record data such as yield and soil moisture
- Simple sorting and basic processing such as bagging grain

**Where AI augments the role:**

- Using satellite or drone imagery to analyze crop growth conditions, optimizing fertilization and irrigation timing
- Develop precise irrigation and pesticide plans using AI prediction models
- Use autonomous farming equipment to improve operational accuracy and efficiency, reducing resource waste
- Use IoT sensors to monitor soil and weather data in real time to aid decision-making

**Human moat:**

- Dealing with complex mechanical breakdowns and on-site emergencies in the field
- In-depth experiential knowledge of local climate, soil, and crop varieties
- Coordinate team operations, train new workers
- Communicate with farm owners and agronomists to develop flexible production plans
- Quickly respond to bad weather or equipment anomalies

**Skills to build:**

- Skills in operating and maintaining autonomous agricultural vehicles.
- Use drones and remote sensing equipment for field monitoring
- Use data analysis and IoT platforms (e.g., AgriWebb)
- Application of precision agriculture software (e.g., Climate FieldView)
- Basic programming or debugging skills for calibrating automation equipment
- Knowledge of sustainable agricultural practices such as crop rotation and organic management

**Key growth areas:**

- Organic farming
- Greenhouse production
- Precision agriculture
- Berry cultivation

## 8. FAQ

**Q: What is the typical salary for field crop and vegetable growers in Finland?**
A: Salaries vary widely based on farm size, crop type, and experience. Entry-level workers may earn around the agricultural sector minimum, while experienced farm owners' incomes depend on profits. Common benefits include housing and produce allowances.

**Q: Can a non-EU national get a work visa as a field crop and vegetable grower in Finland?**
A: Yes, through a residence permit for an employed person (TTOL) with a job offer. The employer must prove the job cannot be filled locally. Seasonal work permits are also available for temporary harvest work.

**Q: Do I need specific certifications to work as a grower in Finland?**
A: Mandatory certifications include a tractor driving license (for road use) and a plant protection certificate for pesticide application. Additional voluntary certifications in organic farming or food safety can improve job prospects.
