# Hunter and Trapper — career analysis · Norway

**Occupation code: 6224 (ISCO08)**

Hunting and trapping in Norway is a traditional livelihood, typically pursued in rural and northern regions. It involves managing wildlife populations, harvesting game for meat and fur, and contributing to ecosystem balance. The occupation is heavily regulated, requiring licenses and adherence to strict quotas. Most hunters are self-employed or combine hunting with other work, as it rarely provides a full-time income. The sector faces challenges from urbanization and changing attitudes toward animal use, but sustainable hunting remains important for wildlife management and rural culture.

## 5. Future outlook / AI exposure

**Future prospect: low (★☆☆☆☆).**
**AI replacement risk: low (★☆☆☆☆).**

Career progression is largely horizontal; hunters may expand into guiding, wildlife photography, or conservation work. Some become certified hunting instructors or wildlife managers. The path often involves starting as a hobbyist, obtaining necessary licenses, and building a reputation. Networking within local hunting associations is crucial. Advancement to full-time status depends on securing land leases and licenses. There is little formal promotion structure; success relies on entrepreneurial skill and deep knowledge of local ecosystems.

**Tasks most exposed to AI:**

- Wildlife population monitoring and counting (via AI image recognition and drone automation)
- Capture data recording and report generation (AI automatic sorting and visualization).
- Predicting animal activity patterns (machine learning models replacing empirical judgment)
- License application and compliance document processing (automated document system)

**Where AI augments the role:**

- Using AI to analyze satellite imagery and infrared camera data to optimize monitoring efficiency
- Improves success rate of precise capture and release through real-time tracking with drones and AI.
- Develop science-based wildlife management strategies using data analysis tools
- Using VR to train on trap setting and safe operation in complex environments

**Human moat:**

- Physical and manual skills for setting and maintaining field traps
- Quick decision-making for unexpected situations (e.g., animal injuries, bad weather)
- Interpersonal communication and negotiation with local communities and landowners.
- Responsibility for ecological ethics judgement and compliance operations

**Skills to build:**

- Drone operation and maintenance
- GIS and remote sensing data analysis
- Ecological modeling and population statistics
- Basic data analysis using Python or R
- Wildlife conservation regulations and ethics
- Project management and basic business skills

**Key growth areas:**

## 8. FAQ

**Q: What is the typical income for a hunter and trapper in Norway?**
A: Income is highly variable and often low. Most hunters earn less than 300,000 NOK annually, often combining hunting with other jobs. Full-time professional hunters are rare; guiding and wildlife management may increase earnings.

**Q: Can a non-EEA national get a work visa as a hunter in Norway?**
A: No. The occupation does not qualify for a skilled worker permit because it does not require higher education and the salary is below the threshold. Non-EEA nationals cannot obtain a work visa for hunting or trapping.

**Q: Is hunting a full-time occupation in Norway?**
A: Very few people work as full-time hunters. Most hunters are hobbyists or combine hunting with other rural professions like farming, forestry, or tourism. Professional opportunities exist primarily in guided hunting and wildlife management.
