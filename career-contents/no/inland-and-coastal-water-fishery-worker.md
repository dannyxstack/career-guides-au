# Inland and Coastal Waters Fishery Worker — career analysis · Norway

**Occupation code: 6222 (ISCO08)**

Inland and coastal waters fishery workers in Norway fish in lakes, rivers, and near-shore marine areas using nets, traps, lines, or small boats. They are often self-employed or part of family-owned businesses, playing a vital role in local food supply and supporting tourism through catch-and-release or guided fishing. The sector faces challenges from strict quotas, competition with aquaculture, and an aging workforce, but remains culturally significant along Norway's long coastline and in inland regions like Finnmark and Telemark.

Employment in inland and coastal fisheries is expected to decline moderately over the next decade due to reduced fish stocks, stricter regulations, and the dominance of large-scale aquaculture. While replacement demand exists as older workers retire, new entrants are limited. Opportunities may arise in niche markets like organic or local-certified catches, as well as in fishery-related tourism and conservation projects. Overall, the occupation is not high-growth but remains stable for those with established operations.

## 5. Future outlook / AI exposure

**Future prospect: low (★☆☆☆☆).**
**AI replacement risk: low (★☆☆☆☆).**

Career progression typically begins as a deckhand or helper, advancing to vessel captain or owner-operator after gaining experience and necessary certifications. Many workers diversify into aquaculture, fish processing, or tourism (fishing guides). Formal vocational training (fagbrev) in fisheries is increasingly valued, but practical experience remains key. Some move into fisheries management or inspection roles with the Directorate of Fisheries. The occupation is often seasonal, requiring flexibility.

**Tasks most exposed to AI:**

- Manual net casting and retrieval operations: automatic nets and robotic arms can handle this
- Manual sorting and weighing of catch: machine vision and automatic grading systems
- Manual navigation and obstacle avoidance for fishing vessels: GPS and autopilot systems
- Visual monitoring of fishery resources: drones and remote sensing technology
- Hand repair of fishing gear: automatic repair equipment

**Where AI augments the role:**

- Using drone and satellite data to identify fish locations, improving fishing efficiency
- Real-time monitoring of environmental parameters such as water quality and temperature via IoT sensors
- Use data analysis to optimize navigation routes and working hours
- Using machine vision to assist in catch quality grading and inventory management
- Using digital platforms for sustainable fishery certification and traceability

**Human moat:**

- Ability to repair and make emergency decisions for sudden mechanical failures
- Safe judgment and operations under complex weather and sea conditions
- Local ecological knowledge and traditional fishing experience
- Compliance communication with regulatory authorities and environmental organizations
- Manual processing of high-value catch (e.g., rare fish)

**Skills to build:**

- Automated fishing gear and drone inspection operations
- Fisheries Data Collection and Analysis (Python/R)
- Applications of GIS and remote sensing technology in fisheries
- Fisheries machinery repair and basic electrical skills
- Knowledge of sustainable fisheries management and regulations
- Aquaculture and Resource Monitoring Technology

**Key growth areas:**

- Sustainable fisheries
- Coastal tourism
- Aquaculture synergy
- Local food systems

## 8. FAQ

**Q: How much do inland and coastal fishery workers earn in Norway?**
A: Salaries are highly variable and often based on a share of the catch. Full-time workers typically earn less than the national average, with many supplementing income through tourism or aquaculture. Exact figures depend on season, vessel size, and catch volumes.

**Q: Can a non-EEA national become a fishery worker in Norway?**
A: Generally, only EU/EEA nationals have free access. Non-EEA nationals may obtain a seasonal work permit for up to 6 months per year, but permanent residency or family immigration through this occupation is unlikely due to low skill requirements.

**Q: Is formal education necessary to become a fishery worker?**
A: No, practical experience is often sufficient. However, a vocational certificate (fagbrev) in fisheries is increasingly valued for safety and advancement, and is free through public vocational schools.
