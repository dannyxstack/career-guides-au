# Midwifery Professionals — career analysis · Norway

**Occupation code: 2222 (ISCO08)**

Midwifery professionals in Norway are integral to the public healthcare system, providing antenatal, intrapartum, and postnatal care. They work in hospitals, birth centers, and community clinics, often as part of multidisciplinary teams. The Norwegian healthcare sector emphasizes natural birth practices and low intervention rates. Midwives also offer family planning and gynecological services. With an aging workforce and regional shortages, demand is steady, particularly in northern Norway. The occupation requires authorization from the Norwegian Directorate of Health, mandatory for all practicing midwives.

## 3. Job demand / competition / intensity

**Job demand: high (★★★★☆).** National workforce approx. 2,937. Employment outlook for midwives in Norway is positive, driven by a persistent shortage in rural and sparsely populated areas. Urban hospitals face less shortage but high competition. The government is investing in midwifery services to improve maternal health outcomes and reduce regional disparities. With a retirement wave among current midwives, new graduates will find opportunities, though Norwegian language proficiency is a barrier for foreign-trained professionals. Digital health innovations are also shaping the role, with tele-midwifery emerging in remote regions.

**Competition: low (★½☆☆☆).**
**Work intensity: medium-high (★★★½☆).**

## 5. Future outlook / AI exposure

**Future prospect: medium-high (★★★★☆).**
**AI replacement risk: medium (★★½☆☆).**

Career progression for midwives in Norway includes specialization in areas like clinical midwifery, neonatal care, or women's health research. Many midwives advance to leadership roles such as head of department or senior clinical advisor. There is also a path to advanced practice roles, including nurse-midwife or clinical specialist, often requiring a master's degree. Some midwives move into teaching or public health policy. Continuous professional development is encouraged, and experienced midwives may take on supervisory roles in education and training of new midwives.

**Tasks most exposed to AI:**

- Automatic entry and organization of routine prenatal electronic records
- Guideline-based routine pregnancy risk assessment report generation
- Automatic Analysis of Birth Monitoring Data and Anomaly Alerts

- Automatic Filling and Coding of Standardized Descriptions in Medical Records

**Where AI augments the role:**

**Human moat:**

- Manual assistance maneuvers and emergency judgment in complex deliveries
- Emotional communication, psychological comfort, and education with mothers and families
- Clinical Leadership and Decision-Making in Cross-Disciplinary Team Collaboration
- Rapid clinical reasoning in unpredictable obstetric emergencies
- Sensitive understanding of maternal cultural background and personal preferences

**Skills to build:**

## 6. Migration pathways / PR

As an EEA country, Norway grants skilled-worker residence permits to qualified professionals who hold a concrete job offer meeting local pay and qualification rules; long-term residence can follow. Requirements are set nationally and change over time.

## 8. FAQ

**Q: What is the typical salary range for a midwife in Norway?**
A: Salaries for midwives are generally in line with other healthcare professionals, meeting the skilled worker residence permit threshold. Actual earnings vary by experience and location, but the occupation offers competitive compensation.

**Q: Do I need a job offer to immigrate as a midwife?**
A: For non-EEA nationals, a job offer is required for the Skilled Worker permit. EU/EEA nationals can work without a permit but need to register.

**Q: How long does it take to get authorization as a midwife in Norway?**
A: The process can take several months, depending on documentation and language test results. It is advisable to start the authorization process well in advance of planned employment.
