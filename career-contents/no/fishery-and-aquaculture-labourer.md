# Fishery and Aquaculture Labourer — career analysis · Norway

**Occupation code: 9216 (ISCO08)**

Fishery and aquaculture labourers perform manual tasks in fish farming, fishing operations, and seafood processing. In Norway, this occupation is concentrated in coastal regions and is vital to a key export industry. Most workers are employed by fish farms or fishing vessels, with tasks including feeding fish, net maintenance, harvesting, and basic processing. The workforce is predominantly male and often seasonal, with many workers from EU/EEA countries. Automation and consolidation are increasing, but demand for labour remains stable due to Norway's large aquaculture sector.

## 5. Future outlook / AI exposure

**Future prospect: low (★½☆☆☆).**
**AI replacement risk: very low (½☆☆☆☆).**

Career progression is limited without further training. Labourers can advance to skilled positions such as fish farm technician, vessel skipper (with appropriate certificates), or shift supervisor. Many workers move between seasonal jobs or combine fishing with other coastal employment. Formal vocational education (e.g., aquaculture operator) enables faster progression. Some experienced workers become self-employed or start small-scale operations.

**Tasks most exposed to AI:**

- Automated feeding systems take over scheduled and quantitative feeding tasks
- Water quality sensors automatically monitor and record parameters like pH and dissolved oxygen.
- Drones or underwater robots automatically inspect fish cages, replacing manual visual inspection
- AI video analysis automatically counts fish schools and detects diseases or abnormal behavior
- Environmental control systems (e.g., aerators) automatically start/stop based on sensor data

**Where AI augments the role:**

- AI-assisted water quality data analysis to predict algal blooms or disease outbreaks, guiding preventive measures
- Automated feeding systems optimize feeding strategies based on fish activity data to improve feed conversion rates
- Digital twin models simulate different breeding scenarios to assist production planning
- Wearable devices monitor worker health to prevent heatstroke or fatigue
- AI document assistant automatically generates farming logs and compliance reports

**Human moat:**

- On-site response capability for sudden equipment failures, extreme weather, and other abnormal conditions
- Manual experience-based judgement of fish and shrimp behaviour and health (e.g., abnormal swimming, colour changes)
- Interpersonal and negotiation skills in coordinating with suppliers, veterinarians, and regulatory bodies
- On-site safety judgment and emergency rescue (e.g., drowning, electric shock)

**Skills to build:**

- Learn to use mainstream smart aquaculture platforms (e.g., AquaManager, eFishery)
- Basic data processing and analysis skills (Excel or simple BI tools)
- Basic skills in operating and maintaining automatic feeding systems, drones, and ROVs.
- Understand working principles of aquaculture Internet of Things (IoT) sensors
- Sustainable farming principles (e.g., antibiotic reduction, carbon footprint accounting)
- English communication skills to suit international companies or export requirements

**Key growth areas:**

- Aquaculture expansion
- Sustainable fishing
- Seafood processing automation
- Coastal rural employment

## 8. FAQ

**Q: What is the typical salary for a fishery and aquaculture labourer in Norway?**
A: Annual salaries typically range from NOK 350,000 to 450,000, depending on experience, region, and employer. Overtime and bonuses for offshore work can increase earnings. Pay is lower than skilled occupations and often fails to meet the threshold for skilled worker permits.

**Q: Can a non-EU/EEA national obtain a work permit as a fishery labourer?**
A: Generally no, because the occupation is not classified as skilled. The skilled worker permit requires relevant vocational training or a bachelor's degree and a salary above NOK 500,000. Most fishery labourers come from EU/EEA countries under free movement.

**Q: Is any formal education required to start?**
A: No formal education is required; on-the-job training is common. However, completing a vocational course in aquaculture or fisheries (e.g., at a county college) can improve job prospects and lead to higher pay.
